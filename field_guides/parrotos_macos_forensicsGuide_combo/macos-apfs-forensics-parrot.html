0How to use this manual

This is an anchor document, not a course. It assumes you already know what a filesystem is, why you hash evidence, and roughly what you are looking for. What it gives you is the thing that is genuinely hard to hold in your head: which command, against which artifact, on which path, under which macOS version — plus the awkward Linux-side caveats that most macOS-centric guides skip because they assume you are sitting in front of a Mac.

You are not. You are in Parrot OS, which means every convenience Apple builds into log, plutil, mdls, codesign and fs_usage is unavailable to you. Roughly a third of this manual exists to route around that fact.

Reading order

Parts I–V are the conventional path and are ordered the way an examination actually runs: get the image mounted, understand the filesystem you are looking at, run the classical toolchain, build a timeline. Parts VI–VIII are the reference core — the artifact and persistence maps you will come back to repeatedly. Part IX is the interesting part and assumes everything before it.

If you are mid-case and just need a path or a command, skip to Appendix B (artifact paths), Appendix D (SQLite queries) or Appendix E (command card). The PDF has a navigable bookmark outline for exactly this reason.

1Acquisition recap

Kept deliberately short — you have done it. What follows is the shape of the process for the record, plus the parts that materially affect analysis.

1.1 The sequence

# --- boot the subject Mac into recoveryOS ---
# Apple Silicon : hold the power button until "Loading startup options"
# Intel/T2      : Cmd-R at chime
# then Utilities > Terminal

csrutil disable            # SIP off — needed for raw block access to disk0
csrutil status             # confirm: "System Integrity Protection status: disabled"

diskutil list              # identify the physical device, NOT the synthesised container

# image the whole physical device to external media
dd if=/dev/rdisk0 of=/Volumes/EVID/mbp-001.dd bs=8m conv=noerror,sync status=progress

# hash source and image, then compare — this is the number that goes in the report
shasum -a 256 /dev/rdisk0
shasum -a 256 /Volumes/EVID/mbp-001.dd
● NOTE — WHY /dev/rdisk0

The r prefix is the raw character device: unbuffered, and on macOS materially faster than the buffered /dev/disk0. Use disk0, not disk1s… — you want the physical device with its GPT and the entire APFS container, not one synthesised volume. Imaging a single volume throws away the container superblocks, the checkpoint area and every other volume, which between them are the subject of half this manual.

1.2 Two Apple Silicon facts that shape the whole examination

The internal SSD is always hardware-encrypted, and you cannot image it out of the machine. On T2 and Apple Silicon Macs the NVMe is encrypted at rest with a key held in the Secure Enclave. Desolder the chip or pull it into a write-blocker and you get noise. The only way to a usable image is to read the block device from a booted recoveryOS on that specific machine, where the SEP transparently decrypts on the read path. This is why the acquisition is done the way it is, and it is worth a sentence in your report — it explains why the image was not taken with a hardware write-blocker.

FileVault is a second, software layer on top of that. Because the SEP layer is transparent, what lands in your .dd is a normal-looking APFS container — but the Data volume inside it is still encrypted by FileVault at the APFS level, with the keys wrapped in the container's keybags. That is the layer you will be unwrapping with the user password in §9. It is also why running strings across the raw image returns almost nothing useful until you unlock.

▲ DEVIL — DISABLING SIP TOUCHES THE MACHINE

csrutil disable writes to NVRAM and, on Apple Silicon, alters the LocalPolicy on the Preboot volume — which means the act of acquisition left artifacts on the evidence. This is expected and defensible, but you must be able to point at it and say that was me, at that time. Note the exact time you disabled SIP; when you later analyse the Preboot volume (§49) and the unified log, your own footprints will be sitting there. Examiners have mistaken their own LocalPolicy modification for attacker activity more than once.

1.3 What you should also have captured

If the whole physical device was imaged, you have all of these already. Confirm:

  • The complete APFS container — all volumes, not just Data. Preboot and Recovery matter (§49); the VM volume matters (§50).
  • Any secondary internal device — on a Fusion setup, the SSD tier and the HDD tier form one logical container and are useless apart.
  • A photograph or note of the machine state — powered on or off, lid, connected peripherals, whether FileVault was already unlocked.
■ CAUTION — WHAT DEAD-BOX COSTS YOU

A powered-off acquisition permanently forfeits: RAM (and with it running malware, injected code, and the unwrapped volume key), the contents of /private/var/folders per-boot temporary directories, established network connections, and anything that lived only in a running process. If the machine was live and you shut it down, say so plainly in the report — that decision is frequently the single largest gap in an examination. §59 covers what this image genuinely cannot answer.

2Evidence intake on the Parrot workstation

The image has arrived on the analysis box. Before anything touches it, three things happen: verify, immobilise, and record.

2.1 Re-verify the hash

The hash you compare against is the one taken on the subject machine. Transfer corrupts; drives fail silently; USB enclosures lie. Verify on arrival, and record that you did.

# Parrot side — must match the shasum -a 256 taken during acquisition
sha256sum /evidence/mbp-001.dd | tee /evidence/mbp-001.dd.sha256
c3f1...9ab2  /evidence/mbp-001.dd

# if you have the acquisition hash in a file, verify mechanically rather than by eye
sha256sum -c /evidence/mbp-001.dd.sha256
/evidence/mbp-001.dd: OK
▸ TIP — HASH ONCE, IN PARALLEL

A single pass over a 1 TB image costs real time. Compute every digest you will ever want in one read rather than three:

tee < /evidence/mbp-001.dd \
  >(sha256sum > sha256.txt) \
  >(sha1sum   > sha1.txt) \
  >(md5sum    > md5.txt) > /dev/null

MD5 and SHA-1 are there only because opposing counsel, older tools and NSRL hash sets still speak them. SHA-256 is the number that matters.

2.2 Immobilise the original

The raw image is now the evidence master. It should never be written to again, and the operating system should be structurally incapable of doing so.

# strip write permission from everything, including you
chmod 0444 /evidence/mbp-001.dd
chattr +i /evidence/mbp-001.dd        # immutable — even root cannot write without -i
lsattr /evidence/mbp-001.dd
----i---------e------- /evidence/mbp-001.dd
● NOTE

chattr +i needs an ext2/3/4, XFS or btrfs filesystem underneath. If your evidence store is exFAT or NTFS — common on shared external drives — the flag will fail and you are relying on permissions and discipline alone. Consider that a reason to keep evidence on a native Linux filesystem.

2.3 Consider a forensic container format

Raw .dd is universally readable and that is its whole virtue. But an E01/EWF container carries its own hashes, compresses well (an APFS image of a half-empty SSD compresses hard), and records case metadata inside the evidence file itself. Every tool in this manual can read it through ewfmount.

# convert raw to E01 with embedded metadata and verification
ewfacquire -t /evidence/mbp-001 -f encase6 -c deflate:best -S 2G \
    -C "CASE-001" -D "MacBook Pro 14 M2 internal NVMe" \
    -E "1" -e "J. Examiner" -N "Dead-box image, FileVault2 active" \
    -d sha256 /evidence/mbp-001.dd

# verify the container against its own stored digests
ewfverify /evidence/mbp-001.E01
Verification completed... MD5 hash stored in file: ... (MATCH)

To use it, expose it as a flat file and treat that path as the image everywhere else in this manual:

mkdir -p /mnt/ewf && ewfmount /evidence/mbp-001.E01 /mnt/ewf
ls -l /mnt/ewf/
-r--r--r-- 1 root root 494384795648 Jan  1  1970 ewf1
# /mnt/ewf/ewf1 is now a read-only raw image

2.4 Work from a copy, or don't work at all

Analysis happens against a working copy, or against the master through a read-only loop device and a copy-on-write overlay (§4). Never directly. Disk is cheap; a contaminated master is a dead case.

/evidence/read-only master image, immutable, hashed. Mounted ro if on its own volume. ~/cases/001/work/working copy or COW overlay — where mistakes are allowed to happen ~/cases/001/out/everything your tools produce ~/cases/001/notes/contemporaneous notes and session transcripts

3Forensic-mode boot and workstation preparation

3.1 Parrot's forensic mode

Booting the Parrot live ISO and choosing Forensics Mode from the boot menu changes three behaviours that matter:

  • No automount. Attached block devices are detected but never mounted. On a normal desktop boot, plugging in the evidence drive can trigger an automount that updates access times, replays a dirty journal, or writes a .Trash-1000 directory.
  • No swap. Existing swap partitions on attached disks are not activated. This is the big one: a stock Linux install will happily swapon a partition it finds on the evidence drive and overwrite it.
  • No persistent state written to attached media. The live session stays in RAM.
■ CAUTION — INSTALLED PARROT IS NOT FORENSIC MODE

Forensics Mode is a property of the live boot. If you are working from an installed Parrot system — which most people are, because analysis needs persistence and disk space — you do not get any of the above. You must disable automount yourself. Do this once, permanently, on any machine that will see evidence.

3.2 Hardening an installed Parrot for evidence work

# --- kill automounting at the source ---
systemctl stop udisks2 && systemctl disable udisks2 && systemctl mask udisks2

# GNOME/MATE will still try; turn it off at the desktop layer too
gsettings set org.gnome.desktop.media-handling automount false
gsettings set org.gnome.desktop.media-handling automount-open false
gsettings set org.mate.media-handling automount false

# --- make sure nothing on evidence media ever becomes swap ---
swapon --show                      # should list only your own swap, by UUID
swapoff -a                        # during acquisition/intake, simply have none

# verify /etc/fstab pins your own filesystems by UUID, never by /dev/sdX —
# device names reorder when you attach evidence and you do not want a surprise
grep -v '^#' /etc/fstab
▲ DEVIL — THE SILENT KILLER IS udev, NOT YOU

The classic contamination event is not a careless mount. It is plugging the drive in while a desktop session is running and a udev rule fires before you have typed anything. By the time you notice, the APFS container has been probed, an HFS+ journal may have been replayed, and your acquisition hash no longer matches. Disable automount before the drive is ever connected, and verify with udevadm monitor that nothing acts on hotplug.

udevadm monitor --property --subsystem-match=block
# attach the drive; you want to see detection events and nothing that mounts

4Write-blocking and safe mounting

Three layers, in ascending order of trust: filesystem mount options, block-device read-only flags, and a copy-on-write overlay that makes writes physically impossible to reach the evidence. Use the last one whenever a tool insists on write access.

4.1 Read-only loop devices

The standard way to expose a raw image as a block device. The -r is not advisory — the kernel refuses writes to the loop device itself.

# attach read-only, scanning for partitions (GPT on a Mac image)
losetup --read-only --partscan --find --show /evidence/mbp-001.dd
/dev/loop0

lsblk /dev/loop0
NAME      SIZE RO TYPE MOUNTPOINTS
loop0   460.4G  1 loop
├─loop0p1 500M  1 part
└─loop0p2 459.9G 1 part

# RO=1 in that listing is the thing to confirm. Belt and braces:
blockdev --setro /dev/loop0
blockdev --getro /dev/loop0
1
● NOTE — THE APPLE PARTITION LAYOUT

loop0p1 is the EFI system partition (FAT32, ~500 MB, holds firmware payloads and boot loaders). loop0p2 is the APFS container — a single GPT partition holding all five or more volumes. Linux will not recognise it without one of the engines in §6; lsblk showing an unidentified partition of nearly the whole disk is normal and correct.

4.2 Mount options that do not lie

# the full paranoid set, for any filesystem Linux mounts natively (e.g. the EFI partition)
mount -o ro,noexec,nodev,nosuid,noatime,noload /dev/loop0p1 /mnt/efi
roread-only at the VFS layer noatimedo not update access times — meaningless on a ro mount but harmless insurance noexec,nodev,nosuidyou are mounting a hostile filesystem; do not let it execute, and do not honour device nodes or setuid bits it contains noloadext-family only: do not replay the journal. The equivalent trap on HFS+ is journal replay, which genuinely modifies the volume.

4.3 The copy-on-write overlay

Sooner or later a tool demands a writable device — a filesystem checker, a repair utility, a kernel module that mounts read-write only. The answer is never to give it the evidence. Build a device-mapper snapshot: reads come from the evidence, writes land in a throwaway sparse file.

# 1. read-only loop over the evidence
EVID=$(losetup --read-only --find --show /evidence/mbp-001.dd)

# 2. a sparse file to absorb every write — costs nothing until used
truncate -s 64G /var/tmp/cow.img
COW=$(losetup --find --show /var/tmp/cow.img)

# 3. stitch them into a snapshot target the same size as the evidence
SZ=$(blockdev --getsz $EVID)
echo "0 $SZ snapshot $EVID $COW P 8" | dmsetup create evid-cow

lsblk /dev/mapper/evid-cow
NAME      SIZE RO TYPE
evid-cow 460.4G  0 dm      # RO=0 — writable, and the image underneath cannot be touched

Point every destructive-looking tool at /dev/mapper/evid-cow. When finished, tear it down and the writes evaporate:

dmsetup remove evid-cow && losetup -d $COW $EVID && rm -f /var/tmp/cow.img
# then prove the master is untouched
sha256sum -c /evidence/mbp-001.dd.sha256
/evidence/mbp-001.dd: OK
▸ TIP — THIS IS ALSO AN EXPERIMENT HARNESS

The overlay is not only a safety device. It lets you deliberately break things to learn how they behave: run apfsck in repair mode, mount read-write with the kernel module, force a journal replay, watch what a snapshot delete does to the free-space bitmap. Tear down, rebuild, try again. Doing this on a copy of the actual evidence — rather than a synthetic test image — is the fastest way to develop reliable intuition about what the filesystem does under stress.

4.4 Verifying you have not written

Re-hashing a 500 GB image after every step is impractical. Two cheaper checks:

# the master is immutable and read-only — confirm the flags still hold
lsattr /evidence/mbp-001.dd; stat -c '%a %U' /evidence/mbp-001.dd
----i---------e------- /evidence/mbp-001.dd
444 root

# mtime should never move after intake
stat -c 'modified: %y' /evidence/mbp-001.dd
modified: 2026-08-01 14:22:07.000000000 +0000

Then re-hash in full at the close of the examination, once, and put both numbers in the report.

▸ TIP — MIRROR THE ORIGINAL PATHS

When you extract an artifact, keep its full original path under 03-extract/. A file called TCC.db in a flat directory is ambiguous — there are at least two, and which one it came from is the entire point. 03-extract/db/Library/Application Support/com.apple.TCC/TCC.db answers the question by existing.

5.2 Record every command you run

Contemporaneous notes are what make findings defensible. The cheapest possible version is script, which records an entire terminal session with timing.

# start a recorded session; -T captures timing so it can be replayed
script -T ~/cases/001/notes/session-$(date -u +%Y%m%dT%H%M%SZ).time \
       -a ~/cases/001/notes/session-$(date -u +%Y%m%dT%H%M%SZ).log

# ... work ...
exit

# replay it later at original speed, or instantly
scriptreplay -T session.time session.log

# nicer alternative if installed — produces a shareable asciicast
asciinema rec ~/cases/001/notes/mount-attempt.cast
▸ TIP — TIMESTAMP YOUR SHELL HISTORY

Add this to ~/.bashrc on the analysis box. It costs nothing and turns your history file into a rough audit log.

export HISTTIMEFORMAT='%F %T UTC  '
export HISTSIZE=100000 HISTFILESIZE=100000
export PROMPT_COMMAND='history -a'   # flush after every command, not at exit

5.3 Hash manifests for everything you produce

Every artifact you extract and every file you deliver gets hashed, so that a year from now you can prove the file in the report is the file from the image.

# recursive manifest of extracted artifacts
hashdeep -c sha256 -r ~/cases/001/03-extract > ~/cases/001/01-image/extract.manifest

# later — audit against the manifest; reports moved, changed and new files
hashdeep -c sha256 -r -a -k ~/cases/001/01-image/extract.manifest ~/cases/001/03-extract
hashdeep: Audit passed

6The four access engines

Every APFS technique in this manual runs on top of one of four independent implementations. They are not interchangeable. Learn the shape of each.

6.1 libyal libfsapfs — the forensic reference

Joachim Metz's library, part of the libyal family that also gives you libewf. It is read-only by design, exposes container and volume metadata in exhaustive detail, handles FileVault with a password or recovery key, and — crucially — is the APFS backend that plaso uses through dfVFS. If your timeline tooling can see a file, it is usually seeing it through this library.

StrengthsMetadata depth, encryption support, snapshot enumeration, stable behaviour, integrates with plaso WeaknessesSlower on large trees; FUSE mount is single-threaded; less forgiving of damaged containers Binariesfsapfsinfo (inspect), fsapfsmount (FUSE mount) Use it forGround truth about the container, encrypted volumes, and anything feeding plaso

6.2 apfs-fuse — the practical workhorse

Simon Gander's implementation. Read-only FUSE, fast, tolerant, and the best of the four at the things you actually spend your day doing: transparent decmpfs/LZFSE decompression, extended attributes, and mounting individual snapshots by name.

▲ DEVIL — THE ENCRYPTION / BLOCK-LEVEL DEADLOCK

This is the defining constraint of your case and it is worth internalising now. There is no reliable tool on Linux that decrypts a FileVault APFS volume back out to a flat raw image. apfs-fuse and fsapfsmount decrypt on the fly, at the file level, and present you a filesystem. The consequence: for your encrypted Data volume you get excellent file-level access, but block-level techniques — TSK inode walks, blkls unallocated extraction, raw carving of free space — either do not work or must be run through the FUSE mount, where they see only allocated files. Plan the examination around this. The unencrypted volumes (Preboot, Recovery, and on many systems the sealed System volume) remain fully available to block-level tooling, which is one reason §49 matters more than its size suggests.

9.4 If you did not have the password

Out of scope for your case, but worth a paragraph because it explains the shape of the problem. The wrapped KEK blob — salt, iteration count, and the wrapped key — is extractable from the volume keybag without authenticating, and is a legitimate offline attack target. hashcat mode 18300 ("Apple File System / APFS") consumes exactly that structure. The practical reality is that PBKDF2 iteration counts on modern macOS make this viable only against weak or known-pattern passwords. Where it does pay off is with the personal recovery key, whose format is fixed and whose keyspace, while large, is structured.

10.1 Gotchas worth knowing before they cost you a day

▲ DEVIL — FUSE MOUNTS HIDE THE FILESYSTEM FROM YOUR TOOLS

A FUSE mount presents files. It does not present a block device, inode numbers you can trust across tools, or unallocated space. Anything in Part V that addresses the filesystem structurally — fls, istat, blkls, tsk_recover — must run against the image, not against /mnt/apfs. Running fls on a FUSE mountpoint will either error or silently describe your own Linux filesystem instead. This mistake is common and produces findings that are quietly, completely wrong.

▲ DEVIL — THE ENGINES DISAGREE, AND THAT IS DATA

When apfs-fuse and fsapfsinfo report different things about the same object — a different size, a file one sees and the other does not, a differing timestamp — do not simply pick the one you like. Divergence usually means one of three things: a genuine parser bug, a structurally unusual record (an oversized xattr, a clone, a sparse extent), or deliberate manipulation designed to confuse parsers. Record the disagreement and investigate it. In several published cases the discrepancy was the finding.

● NOTE — CASE SENSITIVITY

The Data volume on macOS is case-insensitive but case-preserving. Your Linux filesystem is case-sensitive. Two files that cannot coexist on macOS can coexist in your extraction directory, and a script that assumes Library and library are distinct will behave differently on the mount than the Mac did. When matching paths, compare case-insensitively.

● NOTE — NORMALISATION

APFS stores filenames as UTF-8 and, unlike HFS+, does not normalise them — but it does hash them case-insensitively for lookup on case-insensitive volumes. The upshot: two filenames that look identical on screen can differ in byte representation (precomposed vs decomposed accents). When grepping for a filename containing non-ASCII characters, search for both normalisation forms or you will miss it.

python3 -c "import unicodedata as u; n='café.txt'; \
print(repr(u.normalize('NFC',n)), repr(u.normalize('NFD',n)))"

11Container anatomy

11.1 Every object carries its own provenance

APFS objects begin with a 32-byte obj_phys header. Four of its fields are directly useful to you:

o_cksumFletcher-64 over the rest of the block. A block whose checksum fails is either corrupt or forged — and you can test this yourself, which matters in §54. o_oidObject identifier. For virtual objects this is looked up through the object map. o_xidTransaction identifier. The single most under-used forensic field in APFS. Monotonically increasing, per container, for the life of the filesystem. o_type / o_subtypeWhat the object is, and what a B-tree contains.
▲ DEVIL — THE XID IS A CLOCK NOBODY CAN BACKDATE

Timestamps are file metadata and are trivially rewritten. The XID is not: it is assigned by the filesystem when a transaction commits, always increases, and is not exposed through any API an attacker can reach. This gives you an independent, monotonic ordering of every write the filesystem ever made — one that cannot be forged without rewriting the container wholesale.

A file claiming a creation date of 2019 sitting in a structure with an XID adjacent to files written last Tuesday is a backdated file. This is the foundation of §43 and it is the most reliable anti-timestomping technique available on this filesystem.

11.2 The container superblock and the checkpoint areas

block 0        nx_superblock ('NXSB')  ── may be stale; it is a hint, not the truth
                 │
                 ├─ nx_xp_desc_base / nx_xp_desc_blocks ──▶ checkpoint descriptor area
                 │      a ring buffer of past checkpoints. Each entry:
                 │        · checkpoint-map blocks ('CXB')
                 │        · a full copy of the container superblock at that time
                 │
                 ├─ nx_xp_data_base / nx_xp_data_blocks ──▶ checkpoint data area
                 │      the ephemeral objects (space manager, reaper) for each checkpoint
                 │
                 ├─ nx_omap_oid    ──▶ container object map
                 ├─ nx_fs_oid[]    ──▶ up to 100 volume superblocks (APSB)
                 └─ nx_keylocker   ──▶ container keybag  (see §9)

Mounting picks the superblock in the descriptor area with the highest XID and a valid checksum. Block 0 is only a starting point.

▲ DEVIL — THE DESCRIPTOR AREA IS A TIME MACHINE

The descriptor ring holds superseded container superblocks — complete, self-consistent snapshots of the filesystem's root structure as it existed at earlier transactions. Because APFS is copy-on-write, the object trees those old superblocks point at are frequently still on disk and still intact, simply no longer referenced by the current state.

This means you can often mount, or at least walk, the filesystem as it was several checkpoints ago — recovering files deleted after the last snapshot but before the present. No snapshot required, no Time Machine required. Almost no tooling does this automatically. §41 is entirely about doing it by hand.

# find every container superblock in the image by magic, not just the one at block 0
xxd -c 32 /evidence/mbp-001.dd | grep -i 'NXSB' | head -40

# cleaner: scan block-aligned offsets for the NXSB magic at offset 0x20 of the block
python3 - <<'PY'

BS=4096; MAGIC=b'NXSB'
with open('/evidence/mbp-001.dd','rb') as f:
    f.seek(524308480)                      # container start
    for blk in range(0, 4096):             # descriptor area is early in the container
        d = f.read(BS)
        if not d: break
        if d[32:36] == MAGIC:
            xid = int.from_bytes(d[16:24],'little')
            print(f"block {blk:6d}  xid {xid}")

block    194  xid 214415

11.3 The object map

APFS separates virtual object IDs from physical block addresses. The object map (omap) is a B-tree keyed on (oid, xid) that resolves an object ID to the block holding its content as of a given transaction.

That xid in the key is the mechanism behind snapshots: the same object ID resolves to different physical blocks depending on which transaction you ask about. Ask for an old XID and you get the old data — if it has not been reclaimed.

11.4 The space manager

Tracks free and allocated blocks via allocation bitmaps. Forensically it answers one question that matters enormously: which blocks are currently unreferenced? Unreferenced blocks on a copy-on-write filesystem are where superseded versions of files live. §45 covers extracting them.

12.1 The volume superblock records who built it

▲ DEVIL — apfs_modified_by IS AN OS-LEVEL ACCESS LOG

▲ DEVIL — CHANGE TIME IS THE ONE THEY FORGET

change_time is updated by the kernel whenever inode metadata is touched — including when someone rewrites mod_time. There is no supported way to set it directly. So the classic timestomp signature is:

create_time  2019-03-14 09:12:00   ← claimed
mod_time     2019-03-14 09:12:00   ← claimed
change_time  2026-07-31 02:44:17   ← when the lie was told

A change_time substantially later than all three others, with no plausible metadata event to explain it, is a timestomp until proven otherwise. Tools that stomp all four exist — but setting change_time requires either changing the system clock (which leaves its own tracks in the unified log) or writing to the raw device. Both are noisy. See §53 for how to corroborate.

15Clones, snapshots, tombstones and copy-on-write

15.1 Copy-on-write changes what "deleted" means

APFS never overwrites a live block. Modifying a file allocates new blocks and repoints the tree; the old blocks become unreferenced but remain on disk until reused. Two consequences that invert habits formed on NTFS or ext4:

  • Old versions of files persist far longer than expected. Every save of a document may have left its predecessor intact somewhere in free space.
  • Classical carving underperforms. Files are fragmented across non-contiguous extents by design, so header/footer carvers reassemble them badly. Extent-aware recovery beats carving on APFS — see §19 and §45.

15.2 Clones

Copying a file in Finder creates a clone: a second inode pointing at the same physical extents, with a reference count. No data is duplicated until one side is written.

Forensically: two files with identical content may share every block, so finding "a copy" of a file tells you nothing about when it was copied unless you look at the inode. The INODE_WAS_EVER_CLONED flag records that a clone relationship once existed even after the other side is deleted — evidence that a file was duplicated, surviving the deletion of the duplicate.

15.3 Snapshots

A snapshot pins the object map at a given XID, freezing an entire consistent filesystem state. macOS creates them constantly and mostly silently:

▲ DEVIL — SNAPSHOTS DEFEAT ANTI-FORENSICS FOR FREE

An attacker deletes their toolkit and wipes the logs. If any local snapshot predates the cleanup — and one is created hourly on any Mac with Time Machine configured — the deleted files and the original logs are sitting inside it, fully intact and trivially readable. Snapshot enumeration should be one of the first things you do on any volume, not an afterthought. §42 is the full treatment, including how to detect snapshots that were themselves deleted.

15.4 Tombstones

Deleting a file removes its records from the current catalog tree, but snapshots referencing older XIDs still resolve them. The records are not gone; they are unreachable from the present. Walking older trees directly — via snapshots (§42) or checkpoints (§41) — recovers them with full metadata, which is dramatically better than carving because you get the name, the path, and the timestamps back too.

16Sealed System Volume and cryptexes

16.1 What the seal is

Since Big Sur the System volume is cryptographically sealed. A Merkle tree of SHA-256 hashes covers every file's contents; the tree's root hash — the seal — is stored in the volume's integrity metadata and verified at boot against Apple's signature. Any modification to any byte of the system volume changes the root hash and breaks the seal.

▸ TIP — A BROKEN SEAL IS A HIGH-CONFIDENCE FINDING

You do not need Apple's expected value to make use of this. Recompute the tree from the volume's own FILE_INFO records and compare against the stored root hash. A mismatch means the system volume was modified after sealing — which, on a machine that boots normally, essentially requires deliberate action. §46 covers the procedure.

# confirm the volume is sealed and read the stored root hash
fsapfsinfo -o 524308480 -f 1 /evidence/mbp-001.dd | grep -i -A4 'seal\|integrity'
	Compatible features       : sealed volume
	Integrity metadata:
		Hash type         : SHA-256
		Root hash         : 4f9c2a...e71b
		Flags             : 0x00000000
● NOTE — THE "BROKEN SEAL" FLAG

The integrity metadata carries an APFS_SEAL_BROKEN flag which macOS itself sets when the seal is invalidated. If that flag is set, the system was booted with a knowingly modified system volume — which requires reduced security mode to have been enabled. That in turn leaves a record on the Preboot volume (§49). Two independent artifacts, one story.

16.2 Cryptexes 13+

Ventura introduced cryptexes — cryptographically sealed disk images mounted over the system at boot, used to ship Safari, dyld caches, and Rapid Security Responses without re-sealing the whole system volume. They live on the Preboot volume:

ls /mnt/preboot/apfs2/Cryptexes/
App  OS  Incoming

find /mnt/preboot/apfs2/Cryptexes -maxdepth 2 -name '*.dmg' -o -name '*.im4m' | head

Two reasons to look:

  • Version determination. An RSR changes the effective OS build without changing SystemVersion.plist. If your artifact paths depend on the exact build — and some do — the cryptex tells you the truth.
  • An unusual persistence surface. Cryptexes are signed and verified, so subverting one is not casual work. But their presence in Incoming/, unexpected contents, or a cryptex that does not correspond to a known Apple release deserves explanation.

18.1 Body files and mactime

mactime -b ~/cases/001/04-timeline/body.txt -d -z UTC 2026-07-20..2026-08-01 \

■ CAUTION — ALWAYS -z UTC

mactime renders in the local timezone of the analysis box unless told otherwise. If your workstation drifts from UTC, or you move the case between machines, you will silently produce two timelines that disagree. Set the workstation to UTC (§3.4), pass -z UTC anyway, and state the timezone on every timeline exhibit.

18.2 Reading a body file directly

The format is pipe-delimited and trivially greppable, which is often faster than loading a timeline viewer:

MD5|name|inode|mode|UID|GID|size|atime|mtime|ctime|crtime
# everything created within a two-hour window, sorted
awk -F'|' '$11 > 1753000000 && $11 < 1753007200 {print strftime("%F %T",$11), $2}' \
    ~/cases/001/04-timeline/body.txt | sort

# files whose ctime is more than 24h after their mtime — timestomp candidates
awk -F'|' '$10 > $9 + 86400 {print strftime("%F %T",$9), strftime("%F %T",$10), $2}' \
    ~/cases/001/04-timeline/body.txt | sort | head -50

18.3 Super-timelines

A filesystem timeline alone is thin. The value comes from merging it with the artifact timelines from Part VII — unified logs, FSEvents, quarantine events, knowledgeC — into one ordered narrative. That merge is what plaso does (§20), and it is where an examination usually turns from a pile of facts into a sequence of events.

19Carving and unallocated space

▲ DEVIL — CARVING IS THE WRONG FIRST INSTINCT ON APFS

Header/footer carving assumes files are contiguous. APFS, being copy-on-write with aggressive fragmentation, routinely scatters a file across dozens of extents in no useful order. Carvers will produce thousands of truncated, corrupt, or interleaved fragments and you will spend days triaging noise.

Order of preference for recovering deleted content on APFS:

  1. Snapshots (§42) — full files, full metadata, full paths
  2. Checkpoint reconstruction (§41) — same, for states no snapshot covers
  3. Extent-aware recovery — tsk_recover, icat by inode
  4. Carving — last, and mainly for small self-contained formats: JPEG, PNG, PDF, SQLite pages

19.1 When you do carve

# PhotoRec is the most capable general carver; run it unattended against the image
photorec /d ~/cases/001/05-carve/photorec /cmd $IMG partition_none,fileopt,everything,enable,search

# foremost for a targeted set — fast and predictable
foremost -t jpg,png,pdf,doc,zip,sqlite -i $IMG -o ~/cases/001/05-carve/foremost

# scalpel when you need custom signatures (edit /etc/scalpel/scalpel.conf first)
scalpel -c /etc/scalpel/scalpel.conf -o ~/cases/001/05-carve/scalpel $IMG

19.2 bulk_extractor — the one that earns its runtime

Unlike a carver, bulk_extractor ignores filesystem structure entirely and scans for features: email addresses, URLs, domains, credit-card numbers, phone numbers, JSON, PII, and crucially it decompresses and recurses. It finds content inside compressed streams, inside swap, inside unallocated space, and inside fragments no carver would reassemble.

bulk_extractor -o ~/cases/001/05-carve/bulk -e all -S ssn_mode=1 $IMG

# the outputs worth reading first
ls ~/cases/001/05-carve/bulk/
url.txt  url_histogram.txt  email.txt  email_histogram.txt  domain.txt
telephone.txt  ccn.txt  json.txt  windirs.txt  zip.txt  report.xml

# the histograms are the point — ranked, deduplicated, immediately readable
head -30 ~/cases/001/05-carve/bulk/url_histogram.txt
▸ TIP — RUN IT AGAINST THE ENCRYPTED IMAGE TOO

Run bulk_extractor against the raw image as well as the decrypted mount. The raw image includes the unencrypted volumes — Preboot, Recovery, VM — and any region the FUSE mount does not expose. It costs one long unattended run and occasionally surfaces material nothing else does.

19.3 The unallocated-space problem

# the classical approach — which you should expect to work poorly here
blkls -o $OFF -B $DAT $IMG > ~/cases/001/05-carve/unalloc.raw

TSK's notion of "unallocated" maps badly onto a copy-on-write filesystem with a space-manager bitmap, and against an encrypted volume it cannot work at all. The reliable route is to read the space manager's allocation bitmap directly and extract the unreferenced blocks yourself — §45.

21Hashing, known-file filtering and YARA

21.1 Reduce the haystack

A macOS install contains well over a million files, almost all of them Apple's. Filtering known-good content is the difference between a tractable examination and an impossible one.

# hash everything on the mounted volumes
hashdeep -c sha256 -r /mnt/data/apfs5 > ~/cases/001/01-image/data-volume.hashes

# build a TSK hash database from an NSRL set and index it
hfind -i nsrl-sha1 /opt/nsrl/NSRLFile.txt
hfind /opt/nsrl/NSRLFile.txt 8a5f...c21b

▲ DEVIL — THE SEALED VOLUME IS ITS OWN HASH SET

You do not need NSRL to filter Apple's own files. The sealed System volume carries a FILE_INFO record — a SHA-256 — for every file it contains, placed there by Apple. That is a complete, authoritative, locally available known-good set for the entire OS, already on your image. Extract those hashes (§46) and use them as your filter. It is more accurate than any external hash set, because it is version-exact for this specific machine.

32SQLite deep dive

Nearly every artifact above is SQLite. Treating those files as "run a SELECT and move on" throws away most of the available evidence.

32.1 Always take the whole set

▲ DEVIL — THE NEWEST DATA IS IN THE -wal, NOT THE DATABASE

In WAL mode — which macOS uses almost everywhere — committed transactions live in the -wal file until a checkpoint folds them into the main database. 41Checkpoint time-travel

The checkpoint descriptor area holds superseded container superblocks — complete roots of the filesystem as it existed at earlier transactions. Because APFS is copy-on-write, the trees those roots point at are frequently still intact on disk. Reconstructing one gives you the entire filesystem as it was, with names, paths and metadata, for a moment nobody chose to snapshot.

41.1 Parsing the container superblock

cat > ~/cases/001/06-analysis/nxsb_scan.py <<'PY'
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Enumerate every valid container superblock in the checkpoint descriptor area."""
import struct, sys

IMG   = sys.argv[1]
START = int(sys.argv[2])        # container byte offset, from mmls
BS    = 4096

def fletcher64(data):
    """APFS variant: computed over the object excluding the 8-byte checksum."""
    lo = hi = 0
    for i in range(0, len(data), 4):
        lo = (lo + int.from_bytes(data[i:i+4], 'little')) % 0xFFFFFFFF
        hi = (hi + lo) % 0xFFFFFFFF
    c1 = (0xFFFFFFFF - ((lo + hi) % 0xFFFFFFFF))
    c2 = (0xFFFFFFFF - ((lo + c1) % 0xFFFFFFFF))
    return (c2 << 32) | c1

with open(IMG, 'rb') as f:
    f.seek(START)
    blk0 = f.read(BS)
    assert blk0[0x20:0x24] == b'NXSB', 'not an APFS container at that offset'
    desc_base   = struct.unpack_from('<Q', blk0, 0x70)[0]
    desc_blocks = struct.unpack_from('<I', blk0, 0x68)[0] & 0x7FFFFFFF
    print(f'descriptor area: block {desc_base}, {desc_blocks} blocks\n')

    found = []
    for i in range(desc_blocks):
        f.seek(START + (desc_base + i) * BS)
        b = f.read(BS)
        if b[0x20:0x24] != b'NXSB':
            continue
        xid    = struct.unpack_from('<Q', b, 0x10)[0]
        stored = struct.unpack_from('<Q', b, 0x00)[0]
        valid  = fletcher64(b[8:]) == stored
        found.append((xid, desc_base + i, valid))

for xid, blk, ok in sorted(found):
    print(f'xid {xid:12d}  block {blk:8d}  checksum {"OK" if ok else "BAD"}')
print(f'\n{len(found)} checkpoints recoverable')

41.2 Mounting an earlier checkpoint

Writing a full B-tree walker for an arbitrary checkpoint is a project. There is a much cheaper route that gets you a mountable filesystem: make an older checkpoint the newest one, on a copy-on-write overlay, and let the existing tools do the work.

■ CAUTION — OVERLAY ONLY, NEVER THE IMAGE

This technique writes to the container. It must be performed against the device-mapper overlay from §4.3. Verify with lsblk that you are pointed at /dev/mapper/evid-cow before any dd, and re-verify the master hash afterwards.

# 1. build the COW overlay (see §4.3) and confirm the target
lsblk /dev/mapper/evid-cow

# 2. neutralise every checkpoint newer than the one you want, by corrupting
#   their magic so the mounter skips them. Here: keep xid 214402 at block 201.
for blk in 208 216; do
     printf 'XXXX' | dd of=/dev/mapper/evid-cow bs=1 \
        seek=$((524308480 + blk*4096 + 0x20)) conv=notrunc
   done

# 3. copy the chosen older superblock over block 0 as well
dd if=/dev/mapper/evid-cow of=/tmp/old-nxsb.bin bs=4096 \
      skip=$(( (524308480/4096) + 201 )) count=1
dd if=/tmp/old-nxsb.bin of=/dev/mapper/evid-cow bs=4096 \
      seek=$(( 524308480/4096 )) count=1 conv=notrunc

# 4. mount normally — you are now looking at the filesystem at xid 214402
apfs-fuse -v 4 -p 'PW' -o ro /dev/mapper/evid-cow /mnt/past
ls -la /mnt/past/Users/jdoe/Downloads/

# 5. tear down and prove the master is untouched
fusermount3 -u /mnt/past; dmsetup remove evid-cow
sha256sum -c /evidence/mbp-001.dd.sha256
▲ DEVIL — WHAT THIS BUYS YOU, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT

Buys you: the complete filesystem — paths, names, timestamps, contents — at a moment in the recent past that no snapshot covers. Checkpoints are written constantly, so the window is typically minutes to hours before acquisition. If a file was deleted an hour before the machine was seized, this is frequently the only way to get it back with its metadata intact.

Does not buy you: anything whose blocks have been reallocated since. Older checkpoints degrade — the oldest entries in the ring will often fail to mount because their trees have been partly overwritten. Work newest-to-oldest and expect a cliff. Also note the descriptor ring is small: this is a window of hours, not weeks. For weeks, you need snapshots.

42.3 Detecting deleted snapshots

An adversary who understands APFS will delete the snapshots. That deletion is itself detectable.

  • Cadence gaps. Time Machine local snapshots are created hourly and named with their timestamp. Sort the names; a missing hour in an otherwise unbroken hourly series, on a machine that was powered on through that hour (confirm via unified log or powerlog), means a snapshot was removed.
  • XID gaps. Snapshot XIDs advance with the container. A large XID jump between consecutive surviving snapshots indicates transactions — and probably snapshots — in between.
  • Orphaned snapshot metadata. Deletion removes the snapshot's records from the snapshot metadata tree, but the extents it pinned may not be immediately reclaimed. Blocks referenced by no live object and no surviving snapshot are the remains (§45).
  • The unified log records it. Snapshot creation and deletion are logged. Grep the parsed unified log for snapshot, APFSSnapshot, tmutil, localsnapshot, deletelocalsnapshots.
grep -iE 'snapshot|localsnapshot|tmutil' ~/cases/001/04-timeline/unifiedlog.csv | \
    grep -iE 'delet|destroy|remov' | head -30

43XID and inode anomaly detection

The most reliable anti-timestomping technique available on APFS, and it requires nothing but metadata you already have.

43.1 The principle

Inode object identifiers are allocated sequentially from the volume superblock's apfs_next_obj_id counter. Every new file gets the next number. Therefore object id order is creation order — a fact no user-space tool can alter, because the counter is filesystem state, not file metadata.

   Normal volume:  object id ▲ correlates with creation time ▲

   obj_id  2100000 ─────────────────────────────────── 2026-07-30  ✓
   obj_id  2100001 ─────────────────────────────────── 2026-07-30  ✓
   obj_id  2100002 ─────────────────────────────────── 2019-03-14  ✗  ← impossible
   obj_id  2100003 ─────────────────────────────────── 2026-07-30  ✓

A file with a high object id and an old creation date was created recently and backdated. There is no benign explanation on a single volume: the counter only moves forward.

43.2 Detecting it

cat > ~/cases/001/06-analysis/xid_anomaly.py <<'PY'
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Flag files whose inode number is inconsistent with their creation time."""
import subprocess, re, sys, statistics

IMG, OFF, VOL = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2], sys.argv[3]

# body file gives inode + crtime for every file in one pass
out = subprocess.run(['fls','-o',OFF,'-B',VOL,'-r','-m','/',IMG],
                     capture_output=True, text=True).stdout

rows = []
for line in out.splitlines():
    p = line.split('|')
    if len(p) < 11: continue
    try:
        inode  = int(p[2].split('-')[0])
        crtime = int(p[10])
    except ValueError:
        continue
    if crtime > 0:
        rows.append((inode, crtime, p[1]))

rows.sort()                       # by inode = by true creation order

# walk forward; creation time should be non-decreasing. Report inversions.
high_water = 0; anomalies = []
for inode, crtime, name in rows:
    if crtime < high_water - 86400:      # more than a day out of order
        anomalies.append((inode, crtime, high_water, name))
    high_water = max(high_water, crtime)

import datetime as dt
f = lambda t: dt.datetime.utcfromtimestamp(t).strftime('%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S')
print(f'{len(anomalies)} inode/timestamp inversions\n')
for inode, crtime, hw, name in anomalies[:60]:
    print(f'inode {inode:9d}  claims {f(crtime)}  '
          f'but neighbours are {f(hw)}\n    {name}')

■ CAUTION — LEGITIMATE INVERSIONS EXIST

Before reporting a backdated file, rule out the innocent explanations:

  • Restores. Migration Assistant, Time Machine restores and rsync -a recreate files with preserved original timestamps and fresh inode numbers. A restore produces thousands of inversions in one contiguous inode range — a distinctive, obviously benign pattern.
  • Archive extraction. tar -p, unzip and installers preserve stored timestamps.
  • Clock changes. If the system clock moved backwards, everything created afterwards inverts. Check the unified log for settimeofday first.

The finding is not "an inversion exists". It is "an isolated inversion, of a small number of files, in a persistence-relevant location, with no restore or clock event to explain it". That is what the output above shows: three anomalies, one of which is a launch agent.

44Orphans, tombstones and dangling extents

44.1 The private directory

APFS reserves two well-known object ids: 2 is the volume root directory, 3 is the private directory. Files that were unlinked while still open by a process are moved into the private directory rather than deleted — they have no name in any directory, but their inode and data remain fully intact until the last handle closes.

▲ DEVIL — DELETED-BUT-OPEN FILES SURVIVE A DEAD-BOX SEIZURE

If the machine was powered off abruptly — pulled plug, forced shutdown, battery removal — every file that a running process had unlinked but still held open is sitting in the private directory, complete. Malware that deletes itself from disk at launch and keeps running does exactly this. So does a logging process whose log was deleted underneath it.

This is one of the few genuine advantages of an ungraceful shutdown, and almost nothing enumerates it automatically. Walk inodes whose parent_id is 3, or whose parent id resolves to no directory record at all.

python3 - <<'PY'
import pyfsapfs
v = pyfsapfs.volume()
c = pyfsapfs.container(); c.open('/evidence/mbp-001.dd')   # offset handling per your build
# walk the file entries and report any whose parent is the private directory
def walk(entry, path=''):
    try: sub = entry.number_of_sub_file_entries
    except Exception: return
    for i in range(sub):
        ch = entry.get_sub_file_entry(i)
        yield ch, path + '/' + (ch.name or '?')
        yield from walk(ch, path + '/' + (ch.name or '?'))
PY

45Free space and the space manager

On a copy-on-write filesystem, unreferenced blocks are where every superseded version of every file lives. Extracting them properly means reading the space manager's allocation bitmaps rather than trusting a tool's idea of "unallocated".

■ CAUTION — THIS DOES NOT WORK ON THE ENCRYPTED VOLUME

Free blocks belonging to a FileVault-encrypted volume contain ciphertext encrypted with per-file keys that are gone along with their inodes. There is nothing to recover: the plaintext is unrecoverable even with the volume password, because the per-file key was destroyed with the file's crypto state record.

Free-space work is therefore productive on the unencrypted volumes only — System, Preboot, Recovery, VM — and on any unencrypted external media images you hold. Know this before you spend a day on it. It is the single most common wasted effort on a FileVault case.

45.1 Reading the allocation bitmap

# the space manager object id comes from the container superblock at 0x98
python3 - <<'PY'
import struct

print('omap oid     :', struct.unpack_from('<Q', b, 0xA0)[0])

The space manager points at chunk-info blocks, each of which points at a bitmap block covering a run of container blocks — one bit per block, set meaning allocated. Walking that structure gives you an authoritative free list. For unencrypted volumes, extract those blocks and treat the result as a carving target:

▲ DEVIL — A MODIFIED SYSTEM BINARY IS NOT SUBTLE, AND THAT IS THE POINT

Modifying the sealed volume requires disabling SIP, booting to recovery, and reducing security — all of which leave records (unified log, Preboot LocalPolicy, NVRAM). An attacker who does it anyway is either very confident or very careless. Either way the mismatch between a file's actual SHA-256 and its stored FILE_INFO hash names the modified file precisely.

Remember your own footprint. You disabled SIP during acquisition (§1.2). That does not break the seal by itself — it changes the boot policy, not the volume — but confirm the timing of any Preboot changes against your acquisition record before attributing them to anyone else.

The more valuable everyday use. Every file on the System volume has an authoritative hash placed there by Apple for this exact build. Extract them all and use the set to eliminate known-good files across your entire examination — more precise than NSRL, and already on your image.

51Firmlink confusion and path hiding

§12.3 covered firmlinks as a translation problem. They are also a hiding technique, because the unified view macOS presents is a subset of what is physically on the two volumes.

51.1 Shadowed directories

When /Library is firmlinked to the Data volume's Library, the System volume's own Library at that path is shadowed — physically present, never shown. The same applies to /Users, /opt, /usr/local and every other firmlink target.

▲ DEVIL — CONTENT VISIBLE ONLY WHEN THE VOLUMES ARE SEPARATED

Files placed in a shadowed location are invisible to every live-system triage tool, to find /, to Spotlight, and to any script that walks the running filesystem. They are plainly visible to you, because you mounted the two volumes separately.

This is an advantage of dead-box analysis that live response cannot match. Enumerate both volumes independently and compare against the firmlink table — anything in a shadowed path is, by definition, something the running system was never showing anyone.

52Slack, sibling links and oversized xattrs

52.1 Hard links as an anti-deletion technique

APFS records hard links through sibling-link and sibling-map records: one inode, several names. Deleting one name decrements the link count; the data survives while any name remains.

# files with more than one link — rare on a normal Mac outside a few system areas
find /mnt/data/apfs5 -type f -links +1 -printf '%n %i %p\n' 2>/dev/null | \
    sort -rn | head -40
▲ DEVIL — "DELETED" DATA WITH A SECOND NAME

A second hard link in an obscure location means that deleting the obvious copy destroys nothing. Conversely, if you find an inode with a link count higher than the number of names you can locate, there is a name you have not found — possibly in a shadowed path (§51), possibly in a snapshot. Both are worth chasing.

52.2 Oversized extended attributes

Small xattrs live inline in the catalog record. Once an attribute exceeds roughly 3.8 KB, APFS promotes it to its own data stream — meaning an extended attribute can hold arbitrarily large content that does not appear in the file's size, does not appear in directory listings, and is skipped by almost every carver and copying tool.

# rank every xattr on a tree by value size — outliers first
getfattr -R -d -m '.*' -e base64 /mnt/data/apfs5/Users/ 2>/dev/null | \
  awk -F= '/=0s/ {print length($2), $1}' | sort -rn | head -20
41208 com.apple.ResourceFork
39114 com.apple.decmpfs
 8822 com.apple.macl

Anything unexpectedly large, or any attribute with a name you do not recognise, deserves extraction and a file check. This is a genuinely obscure hiding place and it survives copying the file with tools that preserve xattrs.

52.3 Slack space, honestly

APFS allocates in block units, so a file's final block has unused tail bytes. On a spinning disk that tail would hold residue from a previous file. On an SSD with TRIM — which is every machine you will examine — freed blocks are discarded by the controller and typically read back as zeroes, and APFS itself zero-fills in many paths. Treat slack recovery on modern Mac hardware as a long shot, not a technique. Check it if it is cheap; do not build a theory on it.

▲ DEVIL — ZEROED NANOSECONDS

Timestamps written by the kernel during normal filesystem activity carry a full nanosecond component — effectively random. Across a normal volume, files with all-zero nanoseconds on all four timestamps are a small minority. A cluster of them, in one directory, on files relevant to your case, is a strong signal.

53.3 Working a contradiction

The method, in order:

  1. Fix the claim. "This file was created 2019-03-14."
  2. Ask each witness independently. Inode ordering, change_time, FSEvents, snapshots, ExecPolicy, unified log, parent directory.
  3. Count agreements. One dissenting witness is usually a parser or timezone error. Three or more dissenting, all pointing at the same alternative time, is a finding.
  4. Explain the mechanism. "The inode number places creation between two files created at 02:38 on 2026-07-30; the change time is 02:39 the same day; the file is absent from the 02:15 snapshot and present in the 03:15 one." That is a defensible statement. "The timestamp looked wrong" is not.

58Entropy mapping the container

A cheap way to find the interesting regions of a very large image: measure randomness and look at what does not fit its surroundings.

cat > ~/cases/001/06-analysis/entropy_map.py <<'PY'
#!/usr/bin/env python3
import sys, math, collections
PATH  = sys.argv[1]
CHUNK = int(sys.argv[2]) if len(sys.argv) > 2 else 1 << 20   # 1 MiB

def entropy(b):
    if not b: return 0.0
    c = collections.Counter(b); n = len(b)
    return -sum((v/n) * math.log2(v/n) for v in c.values())

with open(PATH, 'rb') as f:
    off = 0
    while True:
        d = f.read(CHUNK)
        if not d: break
        e = entropy(d)
        bar = '#' * int(e * 6)
        print(f'{off:014d}  {e:5.3f}  {bar}')
        off += len(d)
PY

62.2 Artifact work over the mount

For most tasks the mount is enough and far simpler. A pattern that comes up constantly — sweep a tree, parse a format, emit timeline rows:

python3 - <<'PY'
import os, plistlib, csv, sys, datetime as dt

ROOTS = ['/mnt/data/apfs5/Library/LaunchDaemons',
         '/mnt/data/apfs5/Library/LaunchAgents']
w = csv.writer(sys.stdout)
w.writerow(['mtime','path','label','program','runatload','keepalive'])

for root in ROOTS:
    for dirpath, _, files in os.walk(root):
        for fn in files:
            if not fn.endswith('.plist'): continue
            p = os.path.join(dirpath, fn)
            try:
                with open(p,'rb') as f: d = plistlib.load(f)
            except Exception as e:
                w.writerow(['', p, f'UNPARSEABLE: {e}', '', '', '']); continue
            prog = d.get('Program') or ' '.join(d.get('ProgramArguments', []))
            w.writerow([
                dt.datetime.utcfromtimestamp(os.path.getmtime(p)).isoformat(),
                p, d.get('Label',''), prog,
                d.get('RunAtLoad',''), bool(d.get('KeepAlive'))])
PY
▸ TIP — EMIT TIMELINE ROWS, NOT REPORTS

Have every script you write output a row per event with an ISO-8601 UTC timestamp in the first column. Then everything you build merges with everything else, and with plaso, with one sort. Scripts that print prose have to be re-read; scripts that emit timeline rows compound.

63.1 The shape of a finding

Every reported finding should carry six things. If you cannot supply all six, it is a lead, not a finding.

63.2 Worked example

Finding 3 — Backdated persistence mechanism

Artifact. /Users/jdoe/Library/LaunchAgents/com.apple.softwareupdated.plist (Data volume, inode 2100002). SHA-256 9c1f…4b70. Exhibit 1, extracted 2026-08-02.

Observation. A launch agent labelled com.apple.softwareupdated with RunAtLoad true, KeepAlive true, executing /usr/local/bin/sysupd. The inode records a creation time of 2019-03-14 09:12:00 UTC and a change time of 2026-07-30 02:39:11 UTC.

Interpretation. The file was created on 2026-07-30 and its timestamps subsequently altered to appear historic. The label imitates an Apple service; genuine Apple launch agents reside on the sealed System volume, not in a user's LaunchAgents directory.

Corroboration. (i) Inode 2100002 falls between inodes created at 02:38:44 and 02:38:51 on 2026-07-30. (ii) All four timestamps carry zero nanoseconds, indicating they were set programmatically. (iii) The file is absent from the local snapshot taken 2026-07-30 02:15 and present in the snapshot taken 03:15. (iv) FSEvents records a creation event for that path with no corresponding event in 2019. (v) No settimeofday event appears in the unified log for the relevant period, excluding a system clock change as an explanation.

■ CAUTION — SEPARATE OBSERVATION FROM INTERPRETATION, ALWAYS

"The plist executes /usr/local/bin/sysupd at load" is an observation and is not open to argument. "The machine was compromised" is an interpretation and is. Keeping them in separate, labelled sentences means that if an interpretation is later challenged or overturned, your observations — and the rest of the report — survive intact. Reports that blend the two lose everything when one inference fails.

▸ TIP — THE SANITY CHECK

Result in 1988? You treated Mac absolute time as Unix. Result in 2058? You added the offset twice. Result in 1601 or 1904? You used the wrong epoch entirely. These three mistakes account for nearly every timestamp error in macOS forensics, and all three are obvious on sight.

Quarantine — download history

SELECT datetime(LSQuarantineTimeStamp + 978307200,'unixepoch') AS ts,
       LSQuarantineAgentName   AS agent,
       LSQuarantineDataURLString   AS file_url,

       datetime(ZOBJECT.ZENDDATE   + 978307200,'unixepoch') AS finish,
       ZOBJECT.ZVALUESTRING AS bundle,
       CAST(ZOBJECT.ZENDDATE - ZOBJECT.ZSTARTDATE AS INT) AS secs

SELECT datetime(timestamp,'unixepoch') AS first_seen,
       responsible_path, file_identifier

SELECT datetime(v.visit_time + 978307200,'unixepoch') AS ts,
       i.url, i.visit_count, v.title
FROM history_visits v JOIN history_items i ON i.id = v.history_item
ORDER BY v.visit_time DESC;

Chrome — history (WebKit epoch)

SELECT datetime(last_visit_time/1000000 - 11644473600,'unixepoch') AS ts,
       url, title, visit_count
FROM urls ORDER BY last_visit_time DESC;

Messages — conversations with handles

SELECT datetime(m.date/1000000000 + 978307200,'unixepoch') AS ts,
       h.id AS contact, m.is_from_me, m.text
FROM message m LEFT JOIN handle h ON h.ROWID = m.handle_id
ORDER BY m.date DESC LIMIT 200;
● NOTE — chat.db USES NANOSECONDS SINCE 2001

Note the /1000000000 in that last query. Messages switched from seconds to nanoseconds in the Mac-absolute epoch around macOS 10.13. If your dates come out in 2001, the column is in nanoseconds and you divided by nothing; if they come out absurdly far in the future, it is in seconds and you divided anyway. Test on a message whose date you can verify.

-- rowids are monotonic; a low timestamp at a high rowid was inserted late
SELECT ROWID, datetime(<timecol> + 978307200,'unixepoch') AS ts
FROM <table> ORDER BY ROWID;
